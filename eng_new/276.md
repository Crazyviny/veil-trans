THE TOOLS AT HAND
What you decide to focus on in your game is up to you. If you want it to be
about a series of heists in a futuristic setting, great! If you want it to not
have much action at all and purely focus on characterization, also great!
Form your Beliefs around the things you want to engage with in the fiction
and you’ll be giving the MC fictional flags as to what should be put in
your story. What you like, what you want to see, and what you don’t want
to see - should all revolve around your Beliefs and an open discussion
at the table. Beliefs coupled with putting in some cultural aspects and
technological aspects will help organically shape your story, as well as
allow for a contribution allowing for players who stay quieter than others
to express what they’d like and want to see.
Think of The Veil as a set of tools on top of the framework designed to
allow you to play whatever kind of cyberpunk you want to play. The frame
is not interchangeable and is the backbone of the whole system; change
the framework and all bets are off. That being said, the rest of the tools
are at your disposal to create a setting specific to you. How much you touch
on each mechanic integrated onto the framework will speak to what you
want to focus on and find interesting or fun. This is by design.
States are there to color your fiction and clarify the scene between you and
other players when you trigger moves. The feeling wheel is a tool you may
find useful, or not, as touched upon in that chapter. Just how deeply you
decide to touch on your protagonist’s emotions will radically change the
game you’re playing. When you mention what you’re feeling only during
actions to get everyone on the same page, we’re still getting some great
clarity and understand your character a little better. If you want to go more
in-depth though, springboarding off those emotions they feel, drilling down
into other emotions on the feeling wheel tool provided, could change your
game into a more interpersonal experience.
If you create a setting where Cred is relied upon heavily because your
cybernetics, Armor, and weapons break (or require maintenance), you’ll be
using Cred a lot more than a setting where maybe you’re primarily only in
a digital environment. Or you’re in the ruins of a civilization where there’s
not as much use for a monetary system. Both are supported.
Some players like to underline social dynamics, rather than have it
focus heavily on monetary exchanges—leading to lots of interactions
that typically aren’t interpersonal. The social mechanic of Obligation is
so mechanized to show interpersonal relationships with one another;
quantifying them on paper making them able to be mapped. It’s great
tool to creating interpersonal play between all of the characters. If you and
the people you play with don’t rely on it as heavily as people who really
dig those elements, that is also valid.
